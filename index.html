How I Screen Stocks: A Data-Driven Approach to Finding Investment Opportunities

By Ishan Singh · 8 min read

Finding a promising stock is easy. Finding a promising stock systematically is much harder.

With thousands of publicly traded companies available to investors, manually analyzing every company is impossible. That is why I built my stock-screening methodology around a simple idea: use data to narrow the universe of stocks before spending time on deeper research.

My stock screener is designed to identify companies that stand out across financial performance, valuation, growth, profitability, and market characteristics. The goal is not to tell investors exactly what to buy. Instead, it creates a structured starting point for answering a more important question:

Which companies deserve a closer look?

1. Start With the Numbers

The first step in my process is quantitative screening. A company's stock price alone tells us very little about whether the underlying business is attractive. Instead, I look at financial and valuation metrics that provide insight into the company's performance and expectations.

Some of the factors I consider include:

  • Revenue growth
  • Earnings growth
  • Profit margins
  • Return on equity
  • Price-to-earnings ratio
  • Price-to-sales ratio
  • Debt levels
  • Free cash flow
  • Market capitalization
  • Recent stock performance

Each metric tells a different part of the story. For example, a company with strong revenue growth may look attractive at first glance. But if that growth comes with declining margins and increasing debt, the investment case becomes more complicated. The purpose of screening is therefore not to find a single "perfect" metric — it is to identify companies that demonstrate strength across multiple dimensions.

2. Growth Matters — but Quality Matters Too

One of the biggest mistakes investors can make is focusing exclusively on growth. A company growing revenue at 30% annually sounds impressive. But investors should also ask:

  • How profitable is that growth?
  • Is the company generating cash?
  • Can the growth continue?
  • How much is the market already paying for it?

This is why my methodology considers growth alongside profitability and valuation. A high-growth company trading at an extremely high valuation may carry substantially more risk than a similarly strong company trading at a more reasonable valuation. The screener is designed to help identify that distinction.

3. Valuation Provides Context

A great company is not automatically a great investment at any price. Valuation is one of the most important parts of my screening process because it provides context for what investors are currently paying for a company's financial performance.

For example, the price-to-earnings ratio can help investors compare the market value of a company with its earnings. Other valuation measures, such as price-to-sales or price-to-free-cash-flow, can provide additional perspectives — particularly when comparing companies with different profitability profiles.

However, valuation should always be considered relative to the company's industry and growth prospects. A P/E ratio that looks expensive for a mature company may be reasonable for a rapidly growing business. The number itself is not the conclusion. The context surrounding the number matters.

4. Comparing Companies Against the Market

Another important component of my methodology is benchmarking. A stock should not necessarily be evaluated in isolation — investors can learn more by comparing a company with its competitors and with a broader market benchmark.

For my screening process, the S&P 500 provides an important reference point. If a company demonstrates stronger growth, profitability, or other fundamental characteristics than the broader market, that can make it worthy of further investigation. This does not automatically mean the company will outperform the S&P 500. Instead, it helps answer a more useful question:

Does this company have characteristics that differentiate it from the broader market?

5. Turning Multiple Metrics Into a Ranking

Looking at dozens of individual metrics can quickly become overwhelming. That is where a scoring system becomes useful. Rather than relying on one metric, my methodology combines multiple factors to create a more comprehensive view of each company.

The basic concept is: Fundamentals + Growth + Valuation + Market Data → Overall Screening Score

Companies that score well across several categories rise toward the top of the screen. This approach helps reduce the influence of any single metric and makes it easier to compare hundreds of companies using a consistent framework.

6. Automation Makes the Process Scalable

One of the reasons I built the screener was to make stock research more efficient. Using Python and financial market data, the system can collect and process information across a large number of companies rather than requiring each company to be analyzed manually from scratch.

Automation can be particularly valuable for investors because markets contain far more information than any individual can realistically process. Instead of spending hours asking "which companies should I even research?", a screener can help narrow the list to a manageable group. The investor can then spend their time where it matters most: understanding the businesses.

7. Screening Is Only the Beginning

A stock screener should never be treated as a final investment decision. Quantitative data can identify interesting companies, but numbers cannot fully explain a business. Once a company passes the screen, the next step is deeper research.

That may include examining the company's competitive advantages, management quality, industry dynamics, future growth opportunities, balance-sheet risks, recent earnings reports, cash-flow generation, capital allocation, and long-term valuation.

This second stage is where quantitative screening meets fundamental analysis. The screener answers "Which companies should I investigate?" Fundamental research attempts to answer "Is this company actually worth investing in?" Those are two very different questions.

8. Avoiding the "One Number" Mentality

One of the biggest lessons I've learned from building a screening process is that investing rarely comes down to one number.

  • A low P/E ratio does not automatically mean a stock is cheap.
  • High revenue growth does not automatically mean a company is high quality.
  • Strong recent performance does not guarantee future returns.
  • And a high screening score does not guarantee that a stock will outperform.

Instead, the value of a systematic process comes from looking at multiple pieces of evidence together. The more independent factors that support an investment thesis, the stronger the foundation for further research.

The Bigger Picture

My stock-screening methodology is ultimately about making the research process more disciplined. Investors are constantly exposed to headlines, social-media opinions, market predictions, and short-term price movements. A quantitative screening process provides a way to step back and evaluate companies using consistent criteria.

It doesn't eliminate uncertainty. It doesn't predict the future. And it certainly doesn't guarantee returns. What it does is create a repeatable process for finding companies that may deserve more attention.

Screen first. Research second. Invest with conviction only after understanding what you own.
That is the philosophy behind my stock screener.
